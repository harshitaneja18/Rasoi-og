The Fragrant Bark

Cinnamon (Dalchini)

Derived from the delicate inner bark of evergreen trees, cinnamon (Dalchini) is revered across India not as a dessert spice alone, but as a foundational pillar of rich, savoury gravies, royal yakhnis, and festive dum biryanis.

1. True Ceylon Cinnamon vs. Cassia Bark

In Indian home kitchens, two varieties coexist, each bringing distinct sensory profiles:

Characteristic True Ceylon Cinnamon (C. verum) Cassia Bark (C. cassia)
Quill Structure Paper-thin delicate multiple layers, soft texture Thick, hard, hollow single-curl bark
Flavour Nature Subtle, sweet, refined floral-citrus notes Pungent, intense, boldly woody and spicy
Traditional Use Delicate Kashmiri Wazwan, desserts, herbal tea Heavy meat gravies, commercial Garam Masala

2. Whole Bark in Khada Masala

When cooking basmati rice dishes such as Biryani or Pulao, whole sticks of cinnamon are fried in hot ghee together with bay leaves (Tejpatta), black cloves, and green cardamom pods. The high smoke point of ghee extracts fat-soluble cinnamaldehyde without scorching the bark, infusing the cooking oil with sweet perfume that coats each individual grain of rice.

3. Health & Traditional Uses

Traditional Metabolic Balancing

In traditional Indian medicine, cinnamon is documented as a digestive stimulant and circulatory warmer. Modern clinical trials have highlighted water-soluble polyphenols in cinnamon that assist in glycemic control and insulin sensitivity.
